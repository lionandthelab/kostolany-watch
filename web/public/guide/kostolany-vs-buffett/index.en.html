Kostolany and Warren Buffett: two vocabularies, two different questions

Updated 2026-08-16

Kostolany is said to have called himself not an investor but a speculator. Warren Buffett is known for buying businesses and holding them for a long time, and is usually discussed in terms of appraising a business, in the tradition of Benjamin Graham. Plenty of writing sets the two against each other to decide who was right. In fact they are answering different questions.

Different questions

The Buffett-side question is “what is this business worth?” The object is an individual company, the material is profitability and competitive position, and the clock runs in years. Which phase the whole market is in is secondary to that question.

The Kostolany-side question is “where is the crowd right now?” The object is the market's phase, the material is participant behaviour and the heat of trading, and the clock runs from months to years. The fair value of any single company is secondary to that one.

Both questions are legitimate, and neither answer substitutes for the other. Owning a good business through a down leg can be uncomfortable for years, and reading the phase well still leaves the result dependent on what you happen to hold. There is no way to combine answers on different axes into one score.

The line between investment and speculation is blurrier than it looks

The distinction most often quoted comes from Graham: an operation resting on thorough analysis, promising safety of principal and an adequate return, is investment; anything failing those conditions is speculation.

It reads as a clean definition and blurs on contact. How thorough is thorough, and how much is adequate, are not specified. The same action lands on either side depending on how thick the reasoning underneath it was.

So the more useful distinction in practice is not the label but the question: what am I actually reasoning from? The condition of a business, the state of the participants, or the fact that other people are buying. If it is the third, the grounds are thin whatever you call it.

Where they overlap

Emphasising only the differences would be inaccurate. Several observations recur in both.

  • Following the crowd means paying more. The wording differs; the observation that the most eager moment is the most expensive one does not.
  • Patience lowers cost. Neither disputes that moving often accumulates expense and multiplies the chances of error.
  • Admitting what you do not know pays. Staying inside a circle of competence and treating the market as something to sit through rather than forecast are close relatives.

The common misframe — comparing net worth

The criterion that shows up most often in these comparisons is the size of the outcome. But one person's outcome is a single sample, and a single sample cannot rank methods.

Worse, almost every market figure whose name we know is a survivor. Those who used the same approach and vanished are counted nowhere, so reasoning backwards from famous cases always flatters the method. It is the same error as dropping delisted names from a backtest.

The reason the head-to-head keeps getting written is simple: it gets read. But a comparison needs a shared axis, and there is none here — no record of the two approaches run side by side over the same period, the same assets and the same constraints.

Different clocks make volatility mean different things

The difference that matters most in practice is what a swing counts as. If the judgement runs over years, an interim drawdown is largely something to sit through. If it runs over months, the same drawdown is the success or failure of the judgement itself.

So the same decline lets one side say “within expectations” and the other say “the phase has changed”, and both are consistent inside their own clock. This is why the argument so often spins: conclusions get exchanged without the time horizon attached to them.

One more thing — most people are not doing this full time

Both men worked the market as their occupation. The hours available, the material they could check, the number of failures they could absorb — none of it matches the situation of most readers.

When choosing a method, that constraint binds more often than talent does. How many times a day can you look? Is there room to try again after being wrong? Matching those conditions comes before importing anyone's approach wholesale.

Which side is this site on

Neither. Kostolany Watch does not pick securities and does not say when to buy or sell. It does one thing: describe the state the market currently reads as being in, and publish how often that description was right historically.

If forced to choose, the question it works on is closer to Kostolany's — not because that question is better, but because it is easier to score. A regime call produces a value every day and can be counted right or wrong afterwards. An answer to “what is this business worth?” takes years to resolve, and accumulates far too few observations along the way to separate methods statistically.

Choosing on scoreability is pragmatism rather than modesty. A claim that cannot be scored does not announce itself when it is wrong, and what does not announce itself cannot be corrected.

The choice has a cost of its own, though. The reference labels used to score regime calls are assigned by looking back over data that has already happened, so the labels themselves consult the future. A real-time call can therefore never be as tidy as the label assumes, and pinning the exact box acquires a structural ceiling. That is why direction and the exact box are reported separately here.

Using both vocabularies

If you want both, label which question each is answering. A phase reading does not tell you what to hold, and a valuation does not tell you which phase this is. Blend the two answers into one sentence and you lose the ability to say later which half was wrong.

One habit is enough: when writing down a judgement, write down which question it answered. That alone makes a review possible afterwards.
