Who was André Kostolany — his life, the vocabulary he left, and what cannot be said about his fortune

Updated 2026-08-11

André Kostolany was born in Budapest, Hungary, in 1906 and died in 1999. He began his career in stockbroking after moving to Paris as a young man, and in later life became widely known to German-speaking audiences through columns and lectures. He is frequently quoted as calling himself not an investor but a speculator.

Why the name is still searched

What survives of him is not a track record but a vocabulary. The cycle drawn as an egg, the split between strong and weak hands, the analogy of a man walking his dog — each fits in one picture and is hard to forget once heard. The labels he left for talking about markets are closer to his real legacy than any number.

Labels do more work than they look like they do. Once a state has a name it can be written down, and once it is written down it can be scored later. “The market feels strange lately” cannot be scored; “at the time I read it as close to A2” can. Moving a thought into a form where it can be right or wrong is exactly what a vocabulary is for.

On the word “speculator”

The word carries a bad odour in most languages. But the sense in which he used it reads less as a moral verdict than as a classification: some people appraise a business and wait to be paid for that appraisal, others seek their return in the movement of prices themselves. He was saying he belonged to the second group.

Within that group there is a further line, the one between speculation and gambling, and what draws it is attitude rather than the size of the result. Do you know the odds may not be on your side? Have you decided in advance what happens when you are wrong? Have you accepted that it may take a long time? His repeated insistence on patience sits in exactly that place.

What can be said about the fortune

The search term that most often follows his name is his wealth. Several figures circulate online, from different sources, none of them checkable. So this article prints no figure.

That is not caution as an excuse; it is the rule this site runs on — nothing unmeasured is written as though it had been measured. The numbers on screen follow the same rule. For a market we have not scored, no hit rate is shown at all, and figures are never borrowed from another market to fill the gap. A number you cannot source is worse than no number.

And in any case, how much one person made is weak evidence that their method was sound. The next section explains why.

Why famous-investor stories make weak evidence

Nearly every market figure whose name we know is a survivor. Those who used the same approach and disappeared did not write books or file columns. Collect the habits of the successful and they appear to share something striking — but whether that shared trait caused the success, or was equally present among those who failed, cannot be settled from that sample.

This is survivorship bias. It is the first trap when reasoning backwards from a person to a method, and it has exactly the same shape as testing history on today's listed names and silently dropping everything that delisted. In both cases the missing sample was left out of the arithmetic.

So this site does not lean on anyone's authority. Kostolany's vocabulary is borrowed; how often calls made with that vocabulary were right is measured separately and published. Keeping the borrowed part and the measured part apart means that when something is wrong, it is possible to say which part was.

His market and this one

The market he observed ran on rather different conditions. The speed at which information travels, the way an order is placed, what a transaction costs, the range of assets an individual can reach — all of it has changed. If market psychology comes out of human temperament then that part moves slowly, but the speed at which the psychology reaches the price certainly has not stayed the same.

Which of his observations still hold is therefore not something the books answer for you. It is a separate question, and answering it means rewriting the sentences into an observable form and scoring them against history. That is precisely the work this site is doing.

What he did not leave

One thing is worth stating plainly. Kostolany left no reproducible trading procedure. His writing is memoir and aphorism, not a set of conditionals that fire when something is true. The egg drawing carries no numbers for how long a phase lasts or when it hands over to the next.

That is the nature of the genre rather than a fault. But expecting to close the book holding a usable rule will disappoint. The vocabulary is what survived; the rules have to be written down by the reader and scored afterwards. Without that scoring the vocabulary stays a mood.

Whether a book left a procedure is easy to test: ask whether it answers “what would have to be observed for this judgement to be wrong?” If there is an answer, it is a procedure; if not, it is a perspective. Perspectives are not useless, but mistaking one for a procedure means that when it fails there is nothing identifiable to fix.

How this site uses the name

Kostolany Watch keeps his six-phase vocabulary intact. The call itself, though, comes not from his sentences but from a majority vote of eight published trend rules. There are no fitted parameters, and all eight are printed on screen so anyone can recompute them in any charting app.

Nor does it commit to a single box. A reading across all six is shown together, alongside a plain statement that pinning the exact box carries a structural limit. The more a tool borrows someone's name, the more it should say up front how far it can go.

The name comes from the man; the call comes from arithmetic. Not mixing the two is the whole point. One helps you remember; the other lets you check.
