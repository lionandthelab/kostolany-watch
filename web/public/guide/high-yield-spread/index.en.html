What the high-yield spread is, and whether it really moves before equities

Updated 2026-08-10

The high-yield spread is the yield on low-rated corporate debt minus the government yield of the same maturity: what lenders demand for taking on risky borrowers. It is the price of risk.

What it measures

A narrowing spread means default risk is being priced low; a widening one means the opposite. Unlike share prices, this gauge carries little expectation premium. A bondholder does not care whether the company grows, only whether it repays, so the question narrows to “will it survive?”

Faced with the same news, equity markets react to the growth story and credit markets to the ability to pay. When the two disagree, the gap itself is worth reading.

Does it really lead equities?

A widely repeated claim, and credit stress has indeed preceded sharp equity falls at times. But the same caveats as the inverted yield curve apply.

  • Few episodes. Clear credit-stress events across decades number in single digits — not a sample that supports a strong statistical claim.
  • The lead time is not stable. Sometimes weeks ahead, sometimes coincident.
  • False signals exist. Spreads have widened and simply recovered more often than not.

“It has led before” and “it leads” are different statements. The first is true; the second is not supported by the sample.
