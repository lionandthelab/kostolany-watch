Five common misreadings of the Kostolany egg model

Updated 2026-08-14

The egg is the drawing most tightly bound to Kostolany's name. Search for it and the pictures that come back differ from one another quite a lot — and some of them use different axes from his original description. Worth untangling once.

Misreading 1 — the rate-rotation diagram is the original egg

A widely circulated version of the egg carries an interest-rate axis: park in deposits near the rate peak, rotate into bonds as rates fall, into property near the trough, into equities as rates climb again. It is an asset-rotation chart.

In Kostolany's own account, the axis that separates the six phases is turnover and the number of participants. Just off a bottom, trading is thin and attention scarce; near a top, turnover explodes and participation peaks. Interest rates are not the protagonist of that description.

When the two pictures began to be used interchangeably is unclear, and the point here is not that one is correct. The point is that they are hard to call the same drawing. The rotation chart is closer to a discussion of how to split money across assets; the six phases are closer to a description of participant behaviour inside one market. They answer different questions.

There is a practical difference too. Following the rotation chart requires knowing where the rate peak and trough are, and peaks and troughs are only fixed in hindsight. The six-phase view has its own hindsight problem, but at least the direction half can be scored every day. Whether a scoreable fragment survives matters a great deal in practice.

Misreading 2 — the egg is a clock turning at a steady rate

Six boxes drawn side by side suggest six comparable durations. They are not. Advances tend to be long and gradual, declines short and steep; one box on the way up can run for months while one on the way down passes in weeks.

The symmetry of the drawing means the names are symmetric, not the time. Read as a clock, the egg invites the idea that the next box can be timed — and there is no such information in the picture.

Misreading 3 — knowing the current box tells you the next one

This is the expensive one, and the trouble starts earlier than it looks: knowing the current box is already hard.

Where a leg begins and ends is only fixed once the next turning point arrives. Splitting a leg into early, middle and late thirds requires its total length. Position within a leg is therefore a quantity that requires future information by definition. Hindsight makes the euphoric stretch obvious, but that clarity was created after the fact.

One point is worth making here. This repository scored, out of sample and walk-forward, how well a trend rule with no fitted parameters separated up legs from down legs. Whatever that number is, it is a past frequency, not a statement about what comes next. That is also why it is not copied into this article — re-measure and the value moves, while a copy pasted into prose drifts out of step in silence. The current figure is shown on the desk alongside its sample size and the date it was computed.

Position within a leg, by contrast, carries a structural ceiling. Picking exactly one of six boxes is direction multiplied by position, so once those two are fixed the ceiling on the exact box follows arithmetically. Saying the exact box has a limit is not modesty; it is the result of a calculation.

What those two numbers say together is clear enough: the egg splits into a usable half and an unusable one. Whether the market is on the up side or the down side separates reasonably well from price alone; which of the three boxes within that side barely separates at all. One drawing contains two questions of very different difficulty.

Misreading 4 — every asset sits on the same egg

The egg describes the rhythm of one market. Nothing guarantees that several assets share a phase. That is why this site places the US index and crypto side by side but calls each separately. Two markets showing different phases on the same day is the expected outcome, not a bug.

Related: macro series are not inputs to the regime call. The term spread and the fear-and-greed gauges are background shown alongside; the call uses price only. Arriving at this interface with the rate-rotation chart in mind is the easiest way to misread it.

Misreading 5 — the current position is a single dot

Plenty of versions place a dot on the egg labelled “you are here”. It looks decisive, and for the reason given under the third heading it is a stronger claim than the data supports.

So this site shows a reading across all six boxes rather than committing to one. When the three AI reference marks scatter, read agreement as weak. Reducing all of that to one dot looks better and removes the uncertainty from the screen in the process.

Three things to check on any egg diagram

  • What are the axes? Rates, turnover and participation, or nothing at all. Different axes make it a different picture.
  • Is time drawn as uniform? Evenly spaced boxes look tidy and erase the asymmetry of real cycles.
  • How was the current position decided? It is worth asking whether a quantity fixed in hindsight is being displayed as though it were available in real time.

Why keep the egg at all

After all those caveats, one reason remains: it is a good vocabulary. Distinguishing “early in an advance” from “late in an advance” sharpens thinking more than the single word “up”, and once the distinction has a name it can be recorded and scored.

Use it, then, but print the limits next to it. Direction is separable to a useful degree; the exact box is structurally capped. Showing both without hiding either is the arrangement this site settled on.
