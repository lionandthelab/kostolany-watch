Kostolany's best-known lines, rewritten as things you can check

Updated 2026-08-13

An aphorism is a compression, and compression always discards something — usually the conditions. A sentence with no “when is this true?” and no “what would make it false?” is easy to remember and hard to argue with. Hard to argue with is a warning, not a compliment: it means there is no way to say it was wrong.

Below are several lines widely attributed to Kostolany, rewritten into a form that can be checked. Wording and sourcing drift as quotations travel, so only the generally understood sense is treated here, and no phrasing is pinned inside quotation marks.

1. Love money warmly, handle it coldly

The most repeated of them, and the title under which he is best known to Korean readers. The sense is separate motivation from execution: enthusiasm is fine, but what you actually do should be settled independently of how any given day feels.

Rewritten so it can be checked: fix the rule in writing before seeing the result, then score it as written. That is precisely the procedure this repository uses for experiments — what gets measured, and against what threshold, is registered in advance, and a disappointing result is not permitted to move the threshold. If the bar can be moved afterwards, any result can be made to look like a success.

That discipline has already cost a trained model its place. Run beside a trend rule with no fitted parameters at all, the rule came out ahead, and the demotion followed the criterion set in advance. Without the criterion written down first, the model would still be sitting there as a “reasonably good model”.

2. Buy quality, then forget about it for years

Usually quoted in the form of taking a sleeping pill and waking up much later. The sense reads as trade less often. Frequent trading accumulates cost, and every time you touch the position you give emotion another opening. So far, hard to dispute.

But compress it further into “hold long enough and it goes up” and the sentence stops being checkable. The outcome depends heavily on what was bought, and only the episodes that worked stay in memory. An honest count includes the names that disappeared, and personal recollection does not retain that sample.

What survives verification is narrower: fewer transactions means less cost and fewer chances to err. Adding “and therefore it rises” makes the sentence assert something it never contained. The two differ by a few words and are not the same kind of claim at all.

3. Shares pass from weak hands to strong ones

A description of the cycle in terms of who is holding. This one comes closer to a checkable form than the others, because it points at observable quantities — turnover and the breadth of participation.

It remains hard in real time. Whether this is the “everyone is buying” phase is obvious afterwards and ambiguous from inside, where there is always a plausible reason. That problem gets its own treatment in strong and weak hands.

4. The dog always comes back to its owner

The image for the relationship between price and underlying conditions. What it captures and what it omits are both clear enough to deserve a separate article. In short: the analogy contains neither a scale for the distance nor a clock for the return.

5. Markets are mostly driven by psychology

Sentiments of this kind are also routinely attributed to him. As an observation it is persuasive; as something to verify it hits a wall immediately, because of one question — what do you observe psychology with?

The sentiment gauges in common use are built from volatility, momentum and breadth, which are largely derived from price. Verifying “psychology moves prices” with them amounts to confirming that a quantity extracted from price moves with price. That is close to circular.

So this site does not feed sentiment gauges into the regime call. They are displayed as background instead, for the reasons set out in the fear and greed index.

6. Successful speculation needs several ingredients

He liked to state the requirements as a list, and that list includes not only money and ideas but patience and luck. The exact composition drifts between retellings, so no count is pinned here.

This form of the claim has a virtue the others lack: luck is on the list. Admitting that luck participates forces the conclusion that a single outcome cannot judge a method, which in turn forces you towards collecting many observations. That is a statistically correct attitude, arrived at by an author who was not writing statistics.

Leave luck off the list and the opposite follows: successes become skill and failures become exceptions. That is the standard route by which an aphorism turns harmful.

Three rules for handling aphorisms

  • No quotation marks without a source. Quotations shift as they travel, and the shifted version is often more quotable than the original. Reporting the sense without pinning the wording is the honest option.
  • Produce a counterexample. If you cannot name a single situation in which the line is wrong, it has been memorised rather than understood.
  • Restore the discarded conditions. Which market, which era, which cost structure? Reconstructing them usually shrinks the scope of application sharply — and the remainder is the part you can actually use.

When a saying turns dangerous

The aphorism itself is harmless. It turns dangerous when it is placed in the position of a reason. A reason has to be an observation, and an aphorism is not one. “Kostolany said so” sounds like grounds while observing precisely nothing.

One question separates the two: what would you have to see to drop this reading? If no answer comes, it was a quotation rather than a reason.

Why none of these lines appear on the site

Kostolany Watch borrows his names for the phases but does not hang his sayings on the interface. An unverified sentence becomes decoration, and decoration makes the numbers next to it look decorative too.

What the interface shows instead is how each of the eight rules voted today. Putting the arithmetic on screen rather than the sentence seemed closer to the attitude the sentence was pointing at in the first place.
