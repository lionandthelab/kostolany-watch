Kostolany's man and his dog: where the analogy holds and where it breaks

Updated 2026-08-12

The most quoted of Kostolany's images is a man walking his dog. Off the leash, the dog races far ahead, then falls behind to sniff at something, but by the end of the walk it is back beside its owner. The owner is the economy and the state of businesses; the dog is the share price.

What the analogy captures well

First, that prices move more than the thing they track. When conditions improve or deteriorate a little, prices swing a great deal more, because people try to get ahead of the change — and then give back however much they overshot.

Second, that divergence and convergence live on different clocks. Today's price can move almost independently of today's earnings, yet over long enough spans the two lines struggle to drift apart without limit. Holding short-horizon noise and long-horizon gravity in a single picture is what makes the image work.

Third, the distinction between being far away and heading the other way. A dog lagging behind has not reversed the direction of the walk. It is hard to say “a fall is not automatically a change of trend” more briefly than that.

Four things the analogy does not say

This is the part that matters. The image is good for memory and short of a great deal if you try to act on it.

  • It gives no leash length. How far the dog may roam is not in the picture, so there is no benchmark for when a gap has become excessive.
  • It gives no return time. “It comes back in the end” has no time axis. Whether the end is weeks or years away changes the sentence completely.
  • Nobody knows where the owner is going. The image is drawn as though the owner's path were known, but the economy's own path is a forecasting problem too. Measuring the dog against the owner requires knowing the owner first — which is the hard part.
  • The dog sometimes pulls the owner. Rising asset prices feed spending, investment and the cost of funding. Influence does not run in one direction only, and the analogy is simpler than reality here.

Explanatory tools and measuring tools

Analogies almost always go wrong in one particular way: an explanatory tool gets used as a measuring one. An explanatory tool organises what you already know so it is easier to hold. A measuring tool tells you something you did not know. The dog belongs to the first category.

The test is simple — ask whether the tool produces an answer capable of being wrong. The dog explains everything after the fact. Prices rose: the dog ran ahead. Prices fell: it dropped behind. Prices went nowhere: the walk was steady. A sentence that accommodates every observation is never refuted by observation, and what cannot be refuted is not a measurement.

What it would take to measure the gap

Measuring the leash requires a baseline — a value for “the owner is here now”. That value is estimated from earnings expectations, discount rates and growth assumptions, all three of which differ across people and get revised heavily as time passes.

Which produces a strange practical situation. When the gap looks wide, two explanations are open at once: the baseline is wrong, or the price is stretched. People generally pick whichever of the two agrees with the view they already held. As long as the baseline is an estimate, that problem does not go away.

The translation that slips most often

In practice the analogy usually fails at a single sentence: “it comes back in the end” turning into “so buying now is fine”. The first contains neither a time nor an object; the second supplies both. It says on the analogy's behalf what the analogy never said.

The object matters most. The image assumes the leash does not break, and for individual companies that assumption fails regularly. When a business disappears, its price does not come back. An index of many companies persists because its constituents are replaced — and that replacement is itself the process of removing the ones that vanished from the arithmetic.

So “it comes back in the end” is true or false depending on what it is about. For an index it has broadly held; for a single company nothing guarantees it. That distinction is not present anywhere in the picture.

The choice this site made

So Kostolany Watch does not estimate the owner. It counts the dog's steps instead. The only input to the regime call is price: five moving averages of different lengths and three return signs over different windows, eight rules voting, the majority deciding up leg or down leg. No fitted parameters.

Declining to estimate fair value is not a claim that it does not matter. It is a claim that there is no clean way to score the estimate. A price-based call produces a value every day and can be counted right or wrong afterwards. Being scoreable is what lets you learn you were wrong, and learning it is what lets you fix it.

Macro series such as the term spread and credit spreads are displayed alongside, but they are not inputs to the regime call. They are shown as material for guessing at the owner, never mixed into the arithmetic that counts the dog's steps — which is why the interface keeps the two areas visually apart.

So how should the image be used

It earns its keep as a tool for setting expectations. Two habits actually survive from it.

  • Do not translate one day's move into a change in conditions. A sharp fall today does not mean a business deteriorated by that much. Dogs run about; that is what they do.
  • Keep direction and distance in separate sentences. “It has fallen a lot” and “the trend has turned” are different claims. Merge them and you lose track of what your own reasoning rested on.

Push further — try to read the leash length or the return time out of the picture — and you are inventing information it never contained. The better the analogy, the stronger that temptation.
