Golden cross and death cross: what they are, and why not to trust them blindly

Updated 2026-08-05

A golden cross is the moment a shorter moving average crosses above a longer one — usually the 50-day crossing the 200-day. When the short line cuts down through the long one instead, that is a death cross.

Why those two lines

No particular reason. 50 and 200 are convention. Others use 20 and 60, or 5 and 20. The pair did not win on evidence; it became standard because it was quoted often enough that many people ended up watching the same thing.

It is late by construction

A moving average is an average of prices already past. The 200-day line averages roughly ten months, so when price changes direction it takes time for that to show up in the line. By the time the two actually cross, the turn is already well under way.

That is not a flaw, it is the definition. Lateness buys fewer false signals; faster measures trade the other way. There is no indicator that is both fast and reliable for free.

Three common misreadings

  • “A golden cross means it goes up.” The cross is an arithmetic consequence of past prices, not a statement about the future. Pullbacks straight after a cross are common.
  • “It works in range-bound markets too.” That is where it is weakest — the lines cross repeatedly and the signal keeps flipping.
  • “Indices and single names behave alike.” The more volatile the asset, the more often the lines cross, and the less any single crossing means.

A less fragile way to look

Rather than treating one crossing as the signal, ask whether trends of several lengths agree. A short-horizon change and a change that reaches the long horizon are genuinely different situations.
