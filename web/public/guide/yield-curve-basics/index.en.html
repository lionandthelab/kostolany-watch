What the yield curve is, and why inversion gets attention

Updated 2026-08-04

The yield curve plots government bond yields against maturity. Normally, lending for longer earns more, so longer maturities carry higher yields. The term spread compresses that into one number — usually the 10-year yield minus the 2-year.

What inversion means

When the spread turns negative, long rates sit below short rates. That is counterintuitive, and it is read as the market expecting rates further out to be lower than rates nearby — typically when a central bank is holding policy tight now and the market expects easing later.

Why it draws attention

Inversions have preceded several past recessions, which is why the indicator endures. Three caveats travel with it:

  • The lag is long and erratic — anywhere from a few months to more than two years.
  • The sample is tiny. Only a handful of episodes exist across decades, which will not support a strong statistical claim.
  • Recessions and share prices are different questions. Calling one is not calling the other.

How this site treats it

The macro desk shows the spread with its recent path. One thing is worth stating plainly: this indicator is not an input to the regime call. The regime call uses price only; macro series are background shown alongside it. That is why the two areas are visually separated.
