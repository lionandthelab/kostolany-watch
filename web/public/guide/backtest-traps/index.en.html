Why backtests mislead: overfitting and data leakage

Updated 2026-08-07

A backtest runs a strategy over history. The problem is that almost every backtest looks good, because making one look good is far too easy.

1. Overfitting — solving a problem you already know the answer to

Choosing between a 20-day and a 60-day average by trying both and keeping the better result makes that number fitted to the past. The more combinations tried, the more certain it is that one of them fits by luck.

What makes this dangerous is that it does not feel like cheating. You simply “tested a few and kept the good one”.

2. Leakage — quietly consulting the future

The most common error, and the hardest to catch:

  • Filling at the same bar's close that produced the signal — you needed that close to compute the signal
  • Stamping economic data to the month it describes rather than its release date — June inflation does not exist until late July
  • Train and test windows that overlap in time — returns realise over several days, so the boundary bleeds
  • Using dividend-adjusted prices — a 2015 bar carries a value restated as of today

3. Survivorship — not counting what disappeared

Testing history on today's listed names silently drops everything that delisted along the way. Returns computed without the failures are flattering by construction.

What this repository does about it

  • Purging and embargo — cut between train and test windows, with a gap between them
  • Execution lag — fills are computed at the bar after the signal is fixed
  • A leakage auditor — flags feature/label timing violations automatically
  • Pre-registration — what is measured, and against what threshold, is fixed before results are seen
  • An unfitted comparator — a zero-parameter rule always runs alongside

That last one caught the most. Placed next to a simple rule, the trained model lost, and by a rule fixed in advance it was demoted from the default. Without the comparator it would still be sitting there as a “reasonably good model”.
